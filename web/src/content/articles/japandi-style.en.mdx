Japandi blends the calm restraint of Japanese design with the warm, easy practicality of Scandinavian interiors.
The result is a room of wood tones, clean lines and natural materials that feels more relaxed than standard minimalism.
But if you copy the look wholesale without adapting it to Thailand's heat and humidity, it may only look good for the first few months.

## Colours and materials: warm, not heavy

Japandi relies on colours taken from nature: beige, cream, taupe, and light to mid-tone wood.
A small amount of matt black or dark brown gives the room some weight.

- **Wood:** light oak or ash tones, repeated in several places so the room feels coherent
- **Fabric:** linen or coarse cotton, for both curtains and cushions
- **Walls:** plain paint, or wallpaper with a plaster or rice-paper texture for a natural surface

## Real wood and humidity: think before you choose

Thai homes are humid most of the year. Solid wood on a ground floor, or in a room where the air-con is rarely on, can swell or grow mould.
The practical answer is to use materials with a wood look in the risky spots.

**Wood-pattern SPC flooring** gets very close to the Japandi feel and stands up to water and moisture much better.
For something more distinctive, a Glue Down herringbone layout in the living room
turns the floor into the room's focal point without adding any decor.

For furniture and built-ins, choose easy-clean wood-effect finishes, and save real wood for small pieces you can look after, like a side table or a shelf.

## Soft Japanese light in a sun-drenched home

Japandi photos often show soft light filtering through paper screens. You can get a similar effect here with:

- **Cream linen sheers** to soften the light, paired with blackout curtains in bedrooms
- **Wooden blinds** with adjustable slats, ideal for south-facing rooms where the sun angle shifts with the seasons
- **Window film** on west-facing windows, to cut the heat before it even reaches the curtains

## Low, open lines

Japandi favours low furniture, slim legs and breathing space between pieces.
In a townhouse with a long, narrow living room, try keeping furniture along one wall and leaving the walkway clear.
A large **rug** in a natural tone defines the seating area clearly, with no partition needed.

If you really do need to separate spaces, say between the hallway and the kitchen, a glass partition with a slim black or wood-tone frame suits the style well.

## Details people often forget

- **Mosquito screens.** Thai homes often have windows open for the breeze, and dark screens blend into window frames better than light ones
- **Drying racks.** A clear balcony keeps the room feeling calm, and fold-away or ceiling-mounted racks reduce clutter
- **Bird nets.** If birds land on your balcony, choose netting in a colour that disappears against the view
