Spotting a crack in your new home is unsettling, but most cracks in new buildings don't mean there's anything wrong with the structure.
What matters is **the pattern and direction of the crack**, not simply whether there is one.

## Cracks that are usually harmless

- **Crazing.** A fine web of hairline cracks across the paint, caused by the plaster shrinking as it cures.
- **Cracks along material joints.** For example where a concrete column meets a brick wall, because the two materials expand at different rates.
- **Hairline cracks in the ceiling** that follow the joints between gypsum boards.

These can be fixed at the surface, but ask the developer to repair them before transfer, while the work is still clearly their responsibility.

## Cracks an engineer should look at

- **Diagonal cracks at roughly 45 degrees near column corners or door frames.** These can be linked to settlement.
- **Horizontal cracks across the middle of a load-bearing wall.**
- **Cracks that keep getting wider.** Mark the ends and check again in two weeks.
- **Cracks where you can see reinforcing steel or rust stains seeping out.**
- **Cracks in beams or on the underside of floor slabs.**

## How to monitor a crack yourself

Draw a pencil line across the crack and write the date next to it, then take a photo. If the crack has grown longer or wider after two to four weeks,
it's still moving. At that point you need an engineer on site, and you shouldn't plaster over it yet.

Patching over a crack that's still moving just means it comes back in a few months, and by then it's much harder to prove it's the same original problem.
